## 1. What major forecasters list as top emerging technologies for 2026–2035

### Takeaway
The forecasters agree on a core set: agentic or multi-agent AI and the infrastructure under it (AI supercomputing, application-specific chips, hyperscale data centers), physical AI and robots (including humanoids), trust and security tech (AI security, digital provenance, watermarking, confidential computing), next-generation energy (advanced or compact nuclear, sodium-ion and structural batteries, osmotic power), biotech (gene editing, engineered living therapeutics), and space. Gartner already places generative AI in or near the "trough of disillusionment", while agents and AI-ready data sit at the peak.

### Inferences
- The candidate for "the next big thing after AI" is mostly AI moving into the physical world (robotics, physical AI) plus the energy and infrastructure that AI needs (nuclear, batteries, grid, data centers). Separately, there is a longer-horizon biotech and space wave. All four forecasters put energy and physical systems next to AI.
- Gartner's placement (GenAI in the trough, agents at the peak) suggests that 2026–2028 is the "post-peak" period for AI. That is historically when durable value gets built (see section 2).
- The trust and security layer shows up in every list (provenance, watermarking, AI security, confidential computing) and is a lower-hype, durable skills bet.

## 2. Lessons from historical boom/bust cycles

### Takeaway
Crashes at the hype peak usually wipe out investors, but the infrastructure they paid for stays and becomes the base for the deployment phase that follows. Mass value came years after the bust: about 5–7 years for Amazon's breakout products and about 15 years for the Nasdaq to regain its peak. The winners were survivors with cash discipline and companies that built on the cheap leftover infrastructure. Perez herself argues the ICT "golden age" is still incomplete.

### Cited Findings
**Perez framework**
- Each technological surge has an installation period (speculative and financially driven, ending in a bubble), then a turning-point crisis, then a deployment period or "golden age" in which productive capital spreads the technology through the economy — [IIPP/Medium, "A long delayed golden age"](https://medium.com/iipp-blog/a-long-delayed-golden-age-or-why-has-the-ict-installation-period-lasted-so-long-b3471a0aaaeb); [Wladawsky-Berger, 2011](https://blog.irvingwb.com/blog/2011/10/unleashing-the-next-age-of-prosperity.html)
- Perez's 2002 book treated the dot-com bubble as the frenzy of the 5th (ICT) surge. She later argued that the 2008 financial crisis was part of the same turning point. In 2019 she said there had been "two frenzies but no golden age yet" — [Stratechery, 2021](https://stratechery.com/2021/the-death-and-birth-of-technological-revolutions/); [Wladawsky-Berger, Dec 2025 "What is AI's place in history"](https://blog.irvingwb.com/blog/2025/12/what-is-ais-place-in-history.html); [Burnett Substack](https://michaelburnett3.substack.com/p/understanding-technological-revolutions) (all secondary readings of Perez)

**Railway Mania (Britain, 1840s)**
- Odlyzko calls it "by many measures the greatest technology mania in history" and says its collapse was "one of the greatest financial crashes". It was an investment disaster, but it "provided the country with a nationwide communication network of great utility." He also notes that the mania had powerful, vocal critics at the time — [Odlyzko, "Collective Hallucinations and Inefficient Markets" (2010)](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/hallucinations.pdf); [Odlyzko, 1860s mania paper](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/%7Eodlyzko/doc/mania18.pdf)

**Dot-com crash (2000–2002)**
- The Nasdaq Composite fell from 5,048.62 (Mar 10, 2000) to 1,139.90 (Oct 4, 2002), about -77% to -78% on price. It did not close above the 2000 peak until April 23, 2015 (5,056.06), about 15 years later. On a total-return basis the drawdown was about -74%, with recovery around June 2014 — [Phys.org/AP, Apr 2015](https://phys.org/news/2015-04-years-nasdaq-recoups-losses-dot-com.pdf); [DQYDJ Nasdaq drawdown history](https://dqydj.com/nasdaq-drawdown-history/)
- About 200 public dot-com companies went bankrupt in 2000–2002 (this estimate comes from a lower-quality source). Amazon, eBay and Priceline survived. Amazon stock fell about 77% in 2000 alone, and more than 90% peak to trough by some counts. It then launched Prime (2005), AWS (2006) and Kindle (2007) during the deployment phase — [Jon Erlichman Substack](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-amazon-survived-the-dot-com-bust); [EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/economics/dot-com-bubble)

### Inferences
- The post-bust gap to mass value was about 5–7 years for the companies that won (Amazon, and the smartphone/cloud era from 2007–2008 onward on top of cheap broadband and fiber), but about 15 years for investors in the index. The lesson for individuals is that skills in deploying the technology pay off sooner than bets on the frenzy.
- Who won: survivors with cash discipline and companies that used infrastructure the bubble had overbuilt (cloud on cheap data-center and fiber capacity). Who lost: those who financed the buildout at peak valuations.
- If AI follows Perez's pattern, a correction in AI capital spending would not end the value of AI skills. It would move value from "building models and data centers" to "applying AI across industries," which is the deployment phase.
- Smartphone and cloud: no direct data was gathered. The commonly cited pattern, that the iPhone (2007) and AWS (2006) launched 5–7 years after the bust on top of leftover infrastructure, is supported only by the Amazon timeline above.

### Gaps
- Perez's own primary text was not accessed; all characterizations are secondary.
- No hard data was gathered on telecom/fiber overbuild (for example, dark fiber utilization after 2001) or on how long the smartphone/cloud ramp took.
- No source was found that dates when, or whether, an AI bust began in 2026.

## 3. Skills-demand data to ground "how to prepare" recommendations

### Takeaway
Demand is splitting two ways: AI and tech skills earn a large and growing wage premium (PwC: 62% in 2026), and the physical-world trades that build AI and energy infrastructure (electricians, wind/solar technicians) face real shortages. Human skills such as analytical thinking, collaboration and AI literacy are also gaining value, and employers increasingly hire on skills rather than credentials.

### Cited Findings
**WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 (published Jan 2025)**
- By 2030: 170M jobs created and 92M displaced (8% of current jobs), a net gain of 78M (7% of employment). The survey covered 1,000+ employers with 14M+ workers across 22 industry clusters and 55 economies — [WEF press release, Jan 2025](https://www.weforum.org/press/2025/01/future-of-jobs-report-2025-78-million-new-job-opportunities-by-2030-but-urgent-upskilling-needed-to-prepare-workforces/); [WEF digest](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/digest/)
- Fastest-growing skills: AI and big data; networks and cybersecurity; technological literacy (the top three), followed by creative thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, curiosity and lifelong learning, leadership and social influence, talent management, analytical thinking, and environmental stewardship (on the top-10 list for the first time) — [WEF digest](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/digest/); [The Citizen](https://www.citizen.co.za/business/end-of-bank-tellers-soon-wef-report-lists-top-10-fastest-growing-global-skills-including-ai-tech)
- Fastest-growing jobs by percentage: big data specialists, fintech engineers, AI/ML specialists, and software and application developers. Renewable energy engineers, environmental engineers, and EV/autonomous vehicle specialists are in the top 15 — [WEF press release](https://www.weforum.org/press/2025/01/future-of-jobs-report-2025-78-million-new-job-opportunities-by-2030-but-urgent-upskilling-needed-to-prepare-workforces/)
- 63% of employers name skills gaps as the main barrier to transformation in 2025–2030 — [HRM Asia](https://hrmasia.com/wef-tech-boom-to-create-millions-of-jobs-but-skills-gap-looms/)

**PwC 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer (released June 15–16, 2026)**
- The average wage premium for AI skills is 62%, up from 57% the year before. It ranges from 118% in consumer markets to 16% in government and public sector — [PwC press release, 2026](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html)
- Jobs needing specific AI skills grew 69%, about 8 times faster than the overall job market (9%). The analysis covered more than 1 billion job ads in 27 countries. The labor market is splitting into "two distinct paths," and human skills are increasingly rewarded — [PwC press release](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html); [Euronews, Jun 16 2026](https://euronews.com/business/2026/06/16/human-skills-increasingly-in-demand-as-ai-reshapes-labour-market-pwc-finds)
- Note: secondary sources cite the prior edition's premium as 56%, while PwC's 2026 release says 57%. Use 57% as PwC's own figure.

**LinkedIn Skills on the Rise 2026 (published around Feb 24, 2026)**

- Lightcast estimates the US skilled-trades workforce is short about 1.3M workers — [Staffing Industry Analysts](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/data-centers-retiring-workers-deepen-13-million-skilled-trades-shortage)
- The US needs about 130,000 additional electricians from 2023 to 2030. **Signals for spotting the next wave early (cost curves)**
- Wright's law, named for T.P. Wright in 1936, says cost falls by a fixed share with each doubling of cumulative production. The solar module learning rate is about 20%, with prices falling from $106/W (1976) to $0.38/W (2019), a 99.6% drop. The lithium-ion learning rate is about 18.9%, with a 97% price drop from 1991 to 2018 ($7,500 to $181 per kWh) — [OWID learning curve](https://ourworldindata.org/learning-curve); [OWID battery price decline](https://ourworldindata.org/battery-price-decline); [OWID solar data insight](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/solar-panel-prices-have-fallen-by-around-20-every-time-global-capacity-doubled)
- Learning-rate estimates depend on the period studied: 39% for solar in 2011–19 (ZEW) and 34% median for 2010–23 (Drawdown). Prices also reversed, rising 30–100% in 2021–23 — [ZEW discussion paper](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp21054.pdf); [Drawdown](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-distributed-solar-pv)
- Patents, research publications and job postings are the leading indicators that McKinsey itself tracks to score trends — [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/the-top-trends-in-tech-2025)

### Inferences
- A sound "prepare" portfolio looks like this: (a) AI literacy or AI-tool fluency layered onto an existing domain, where PwC's 62% premium is the best hard evidence; (b) durable human skills (analytical thinking, collaboration, client relations), which WEF, LinkedIn and PwC all highlight; (c) for people open to hands-on work, electrical and energy trades, where the bottleneck is physical rather than digital.
- Percentage growth for new-energy trades (BLS) is high but comes from a tiny base. Electricians offer far more absolute openings, about 81,000 a year.
- Practical early-warning signals: a technology that is steadily falling along a Wright's-law curve (batteries, solar, likely robotics hardware and AI inference costs), rising job-posting share in McKinsey-style trackers, and moves on the Gartner hype cycle.
